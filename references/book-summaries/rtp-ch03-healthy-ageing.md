# Chapter 3: Planning for healthy ageing

## Core Idea

Third places are social infrastructure for older adults: beyond home (first) and retired absence of workplace (second), accessible public settings sustain strong, weak, and absent ties that compose social health and overall well-being. Alidoust and Bosman's Gold Coast qualitative study (54 participants, 65+, observations + interviews) shows third places are not merely physical—they are social dimensions where regular, voluntary interaction produces Granovetter's tie types. Accessibility (reach desired activities) matters as much as mobility (movement itself); car dependence in sprawling suburbs makes driving cessation a social-health cliff.

Use this chapter when planning age-friendly neighbourhoods, transport, or MPC amenities—prioritise mixed-use local third places, club networks, and post-driving mobility before assuming on-site "community" substitutes city-wide sociability.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Social health**: Sociability as component of social well-being contributing to overall health (Greenblatt; Keyes; WHO 1948, 2007 Global Age-Friendly Cities).
- **Granovetter's tie typology**: **Strong ties**—intense friendships within clusters; **weak ties**—acquaintances bridging clusters, "feeling of home," security, practical support (Henning & Lieberg); **absent ties**—acknowledgement ties (nods) without acquaintance.
- **Mobility vs. accessibility (Litman; Victoria Transport Policy Institute)**: Movement of people/goods vs. ability to reach goods, services, activities, destinations.
- **Oldenburg's third place**: Informal public gathering; accessible, socially level neutral ground; regulars; responds to individualisation with "spiritual tonic" against stress, loneliness, alienation (Oldenburg 1999).
- **Neighbourhood built-form comparison**: **Conventional suburbs** (detached/semi/attached, varied street patterns) vs. **Master Planned Communities (MPCs)**—comprehensive master plan, distinct boundaries, uniform aesthetic; age-segregated to mixed-age; gated to non-gated; includes high-rise.
- **Case study areas (Gold Coast)**: Southport (dense, grid, mixed use, bus/light rail); Mermaid Waters–Clear Island Waters (cul-de-sacs, limited bus on main roads); Hope Island (gated MPC, low density, car-dependent, restricted external access).

## Key Concepts

- **Third-place typologies for older adults**: Mixed-use shopping/civic clusters (efficiency, multi-activity trips); local churches, libraries, clubs (RSL, University of the Third Age, bowls, singing); pubs/cafés/restaurants with long-term regulars; MPC common areas and leisure centres (exclusive programmed activities); restaurants as non-obligatory social interaction sites (Cheang; Rosenbaum).
- **Visitation frequency**: Regular attendance at same local centre forms absent/weak ties with staff and regular shoppers—even mixed-use centres rarely create new strong ties but maintain existing networks.
- **Length of residence**: Longer tenure → deeper local third-place attachment and tie accumulation.
- **Age-segregated MPCs**: Higher absent/weak tie density inside development; some residents know nearly all neighbours in 227-house enclave; risk of inward-looking social life vs. those retaining external strong ties.
- **Car as accessibility proxy**: Most drivers define accessible third place as easy by car; non-drivers (13%) define it by public transport availability—perceptions diverge by mode.
- **Driving cessation**: Feared social contraction; Probus, turf club, wife's organisations unreachable without taxi in car-oriented cul-de-sacs.
- **Public transport barriers**: Distance to stops, unreliability, low frequency, timetables ill-suited to retired patterns—especially Hope Island and cul-de-sac Mermaid Waters; Southport retirees more confident in bus/light rail fallback.

## Mental Models

- **Weak and absent ties are features, not failures.** Older participants valued bowling-club nodding relationships and shopping-centre familiarity as daily social health—not only close friendships.
- **Local + mixed-use beats distant single-purpose.** Combine errands and coffee to minimise travel for those with walking limits (~15 minutes; aids).
- **MPC internal amenity ≠ city sociability.** Age-segregated leisure centres excel at weak ties inside the gate but may gate external third places (Hope Island) unless embedded in dense, mixed, transit-rich contexts.
- **Accessibility follows street network and land use.** Grid, mixed use, frequent transit (Southport) extend third-place reach after driving stops; cul-de-sacs and gates fragment it.
- **Third place = physical + social place.** Not a building label—a pattern of regular voluntary interaction Oldenburg described beyond family and work.

## Anti-patterns

- **Locating MPCs only in low-density gated enclaves** without transit to external amenities—residents shop outside but rarely socialise beyond the development.
- **Assuming on-site bingo/cards fulfil all social needs** while ignoring residents who never leave their houses yet pay levies (Ruth's critique of non-participants).
- **Planning age-friendly housing without flexible transport** for retiring drivers—timetabled sparse buses fail sprawled Australian patterns.
- **Overweighting strong ties in social policy** while neglecting weak ties that neighbourhood proximity and third places uniquely sustain.
- **Treating MPC common areas as complete substitutes** for pubs, clubs, churches, libraries in the wider suburb—relocators benefit most; long-term locals keep external networks.

## Worked Example

**Tony (67, Southport) at the local pub**

Long-term regular at a revamped pub; ties span 10–60 years including former workmates. Strong, weak, and absent ties layered through one third place; place belonging tied to physical renovation he even worked on. Use when designing or preserving "place champions" and low-profile informal venues—continuity of patronage across redevelopment matters as much as physical upgrade.

**Claire (86, age-segregated MPC) vs. Bob (72, Hope Island)**

Claire: cards, bingo, hall games weekly—high internal weak ties, fluid seating groups. Bob: Hope Island "inaccessible without a car," yet "very accessible" with car—shows mode-dependent accessibility paradox. Intervention pairing: embed MPCs in Southport-like contexts (density, mix, transit); add demand-responsive transport for cul-de-sac retirees facing driving cessation (Frank, 81, on Probus/turf club/taxi dependence).

## Key Takeaways

1. Third places support social health through strong, weak, and absent ties—most new ties in study were weak/absent but deemed vital (Shirley on bowls club conversation).
2. Two third-place properties matter universally: **multiple services in one place** and **proximity to housing**.
3. Conventional suburb participants: clubs (especially age-segregated) as loneliness antidote (Lyn: "clubs are my family").
4. MPC participants: social life concentrated in common areas/leisure centres; age-segregated developments show highest internal tie counts; external engagement needs deliberate urban context design.
5. Plan alternative transport and awareness programmes for non-drivers; improve stop proximity, frequency, and flexible services—WHO age-friendly alignment.
