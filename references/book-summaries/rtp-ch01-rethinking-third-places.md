# Chapter 1: Rethinking third places and community building

## Core Idea

Third places are not products of master planning—they often emerge where formal planning has erased informal public life. Bosman and Dolley situate Ray Oldenburg's third place concept within a critical genealogy of "community" as a planning technique: from Garden City utopias through New Urbanism and Eco-Villages, planners have repeatedly claimed that built form can manufacture gemeinschaft. The chapter argues that these claims naturalise particular (often docile, homogeneous) subjects while obscuring difference, and that third places offer a counter-logic—grassroots, user-modified spaces where community is "most alive."

Use this chapter when you need both Oldenburg's operational criteria and a sceptical lens on why official "community" developments so often lack genuine third places.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ray Oldenburg's Third Place (eight characteristics)**: (1) neutral ground or common meeting place; (2) levellers—inclusive of social and cultural diversity; (3) easy access accommodating sedentary and active activities; (4) place champions or regular patrons; (5) low profile and informal; (6) playful atmosphere; (7) home away from home; (8) conversation as primary activity.
- **Garden City (Ebenezer Howard)**: Self-contained, self-governing city-in-country; membership through active physical participation; inclusion/exclusion of "us/them."
- **Broadacre City (Frank Lloyd Wright)**: Democratic, car-centred organic city merging farm, recreation, local schools, and inclusive government—governmental space producing "normal" healthy social citizens (Osborne & Rose).
- **New Towns**: Post-WWII reworking of Garden City; social segregation of blue/white collar workers; decline of corner stores and cafés under urban renewal.
- **Village Idyll (Raymond Unwin → Charles Reade)**: Arts and Crafts medieval imagery; gemeinschaft through like-minded residents, local materials, and controlled density—propagated suburban sprawl and social polarisation (Colonel Light Gardens, South Australia).
- **New Urbanism**: Calthorpe's determinates—pedestrian scale, identifiable centre and edge, integrated diversity of use and population, defined public space; Duany & Plater-Zyberk's neighbourhood organisation; community as market commodity.
- **Eco-Village (Hugh Barton)**: "An attractive, convivial and healthy place that balances privacy with community and local provision with city access."
- **Sandercock's recovery**: Revisit heroic planning histories to make invisible stories visible—not to re-inscribe totalising modernist narratives.

## Key Concepts

- **First / second / third place**: Home, work/school, and informal public gathering places where belonging develops through voluntary interaction.
- **Community as planning rationality**: Ideals of cohesion, sharing, and neighbourliness deployed to discipline behaviour—assumes predisposition to interaction regardless of history, income, or belief.
- **Docile subjects**: Garden City, New Town, and suburban village models imagined obedient, homogeneous residents without poverty, illness, or dissent (Jacobs's critique of Howard).
- **User modification vs. planner resistance**: Oldenburg's "triumph of the space user"—people repurposed establishments for other ends; contemporary environments resist such appropriation.
- **Weak ties and social capital**: Third places mediate individual and society; incidental interaction builds social capital, safety perception, and sense of place (extended in later book chapters).
- **Expanded third-place typologies**: Wet markets, franchised cafés, smoking zones, Pokémon Go gatherings, libraries, parks, virtual layers—concept travels beyond Oldenburg's original commercial list.

## Mental Models

- **Planning cannot install community; it can enable or destroy third places.** Master plans regulate daily life; third places arise from patron behaviour, regulars, and conversation.
- **Every "community" development carries an exclusion map.** Fixed boundaries, gated access, price points, and participation requirements define who belongs.
- **Historical repetition with shifted profit motive.** Howard → New Towns → New Urbanism → Eco-Village: same walkable, mixed-use rhetoric; later iterations commercialise "community" for those who can afford it.
- **Top-down plus bottom-up.** Hall & Ward: state regulation and local grassroots action both shape place networks—align official amenity provision with resident-led third-place creation.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming third places appear because the master plan labels a "town centre."** New Towns lacked the spontaneous web of named relationships that village life required (Dennis)—form without informal gathering nodes fails.
- **Privileging car travel and privatised space** (Modernism, suburban malls): produces "regimented loneliness" and adolescent consumership/passivity in shopping malls instead of civic third places.
- **Selling community as a product** without affordability or diversity: New Urbanist charter rhetoric on mixed housing types does not guarantee interaction if residents cannot access or modify spaces.
- **Eco-Village utopian social mix** disconnected from contemporary mobility patterns (Taylor): green infrastructure without fitting existing urban fabric reproduces exclusion.
- **Ignoring gender, class, race, and ability** in "neutral" third-place design—addressed explicitly in Chapter 2.

## Worked Example

**New Urbanist "post-suburban" development vs. Oldenburg's critique**

Apply Calthorpe's checklist (pedestrian scale, centre/edge, mixed use, public space) to a proposed neighbourhood plan. Then audit for Oldenburg's eight characteristics: Is conversation primary or is the "public square" a transit vestibule? Are there regular patrons (place champions) or only transient shoppers? Can users modify the space (add a bulletin board, linger without purchase)?

Contrast with Howard's Garden City: inclusive on paper, but Jacobs noted it suited residents "docile and with no plans of their own." Use this pairing when stakeholders claim "we've built community"—ask whether weak ties form at a coffee counter or mail drop, as Oldenburg prescribed, or whether residents only shuttle home-to-work.

## Key Takeaways

1. Preserve Oldenburg's eight characteristics verbatim when assessing or designing candidate third places—they remain the book's shared diagnostic.
2. Historical planning "community" techniques (Garden City, New Towns, New Urbanism, Eco-Village) explain why many contemporary landscapes lack informal public life; they are context, not substitutes for third-place analysis.
3. Third places compensate for mobility, loneliness, and urbanisation; research citations (Putnam, Jacobs, Mele et al. on wet markets, Jones et al. on franchised cafés) show the concept's empirical spread.
4. Grassroots efforts—community gardens, music archives (Chapters 7–8)—are "best hope" where environments are badly designed.
5. The edited volume maps theory (Chs 1–6) then site-specific third places (Chs 7–11): museums, gardens, cyberspace, public transport, sidewalks.
