# Chapter 11: Third Places and Their Contribution to Street Life

## Core Idea

Farahani and Beynon shift focus from **inside** the café to what happens **outside**: how third places—especially pavement dining—activate commercial streets and suburban neighbourhood centres. Oldenburg's indoor refuge extends into the public realm when eateries break the indoor-outdoor boundary. In low-density Australian suburbs often judged lifeless, cafés as "hearts of urbanism" can supply the missing vitality—but only when specific **physical and urban design qualities** are present.

Their Geelong study (Separation Street, Pakington Street, Belle Vue Avenue) shows pavement dining generates ~46% of observed street activities and ~68% of social/group activities. Third places are **Zone 1** of street life: the first and most stationary activity band on commercial strips.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Third place theory** (Oldenburg, 1989/2009) — Neutral, inclusive, playful conversation beyond home and work; commercial venues as semi-public third places.
- **Flânerie + third places → reinvented streets** (Banerjee, 2001) — Mix of hangout infrastructure and street-oriented third places scripts successful public life.
- **Lefebvre's Production of Space** — Perceived space (espace perçu), conceived space (espace conçu), lived space (espace vécu); places draw meaning performatively from use, not designer intent alone.
- **Mehta and Bosson (2009)** — High personalisation + permeability + footpath seats/shelter → street sociability.
- **Gehl's soft edges** — Indistinct public-private boundaries; transparency, permeability, detail, active facades.
- **Three zones of activity** (Farahani & Beynon) — (1) pavement dining around eateries, (2) frequent services (supermarket, bakery), (3) connectors between zones (mostly pass-through).
- **Three footpath interaction modes** — Claim footpath; sacrifice lot/front yard; or open-front transparency when space is tight.
- **Habitus** (Bourdieu, 1977) — Physical settings casings for action; pavement dining as collective production of neighbourhood identity.

## Key Concepts

- **Pavement dining as street stage** (Oosterman; Whyte) — Watching people is the primary urban activity; chairs oriented to the street.
- **Eyes on the street / natural surveillance** (Jacobs; Oldenburg) — Outdoor cafés increase safety and visibility vs. fully privatised realm.
- **Semi-public commercial third places** — Privately owned, publicly accommodating; meeting enabled through consuming.
- **Weak ties with strong support** (Granovetter; Rosenbaum et al.) — Loyalty correlates with emotional support received in venues.
- **Personalisation** (Mehta, Kopec, Abu-Ghazzeh) — Territorial markers: ads, decor, shade/chairs, flower boxes, vertical filters.
- **Soft vs. hard edges** — Soft: transparency, permeability, detail linking interior to footpath; hard: when exterior environment is undesirable, venues retreat inward.
- **Affordances of the built environment** — Same café brand performs differently on different streets (Geelong West vs. Highton branches).

## Mental Models

1. **Indoor third place ≠ street vitality** — Enter/exit traffic alone does not activate the neighbourhood; outdoor staying activities do.
2. **Zone hierarchy** — Design for Zone 1 (lingering dining) first; Zone 2 services bring frequency; Zone 3 connectors need linking strategies or stay thin.
3. **Width + area thresholds** — Successful cases: footpath/front yard >5 m, outdoor seating area >60 m² for higher patron counts.
4. **Personalisation beats raw width** — Enclosed lot dining with walls can outperform exposed footpath seating if territory is well defined.
5. **Lived space over conceived space** — Suburban café culture (post–Southern European migration) is real and imagined (Soja); rituals matter for suburban social life.
6. **Same brand, different street life** — Non-physical qualities constant; built environment explains branch performance differences.

## Anti-patterns

- **Chain/government displacement** of locally owned steady-state third places (Oldenburg, 2009).
- **Indoor-only cafés on wide footpaths** — Wasted affordance; minimal street activation.
- **Dull soft edges** (Mehta) — Transparency without detail creates dead space, not vitality.
- **Narrow footpaths without lot sacrifice** — No claim, no front yard, no open front → hard edges and interior withdrawal.
- **Under-personalised outdoor seating** — Low scores (e.g., third places 8 & 9) correlate with zero stationary activities.
- **Suburban pejorative by default** — Assuming low density precludes social life without testing neighbourhood centre design.
- **Greenery as mandatory** — Helpful for beautification, barriers, shade; not strictly required if other qualities strong.

## Worked Example

Nine top-performing eateries across three Geelong streets were rated on footpath width, outdoor area (m²), personalisation (high = ≥3 of five types: ad boards, decor, shade/chairs, flower boxes, vertical filters), softness of edges (high = ≥2 of: detail, visual inside-outside connection, permeability), and greenery roles.

**Third place 1 (Highton):** 39 activities, 38 stationary; 5–8 m width, 140 m²; high on all three qualitative measures—model pavement café.

**Third place 5 (Geelong West):** Two outdoor areas—walled lot enclosure full all day; footpath seats with flower boxes barely used. **Lesson:** defined, personalised territory beats exposed pavement.

**Third places 8 & 9 (Bell Park):** Low personalisation, low softness, zero stationary activities—hard edges when the street environment offers nothing worth engaging.

**Branch comparison:** Identical menus and brand; Geelong West branch outperforms Highton branch for one operator, reverse for another—**environment affordance**, not F&B quality, drives street life.

## Key Takeaways

- Pavement dining is the primary engine of **social** street activity in suburban commercial strips (~68% group activities).
- Critical physical success factors: **claimable storefront**, wide footpath or front yard (>5 m), adequate dining area (>60 m²), **personalisation** (≥3 types), **soft edges** (permeability + transparency), boundary definition via panels/shades/flower boxes.
- Three zones structure interventions: anchor Zone 1 eateries first, then services, then connectors.
- Cafés inclusive vs. pubs/clubs—broader age and cultural draw; business transacted at tables.
- Suburban neighbourhood centres are viable **hearts of urbanism** when design links lived experience to street performance.
- Benjamin's "living means leaving traces" + Bourdieu's habitus—pavement dining materialises neighbourhood identity over time.
