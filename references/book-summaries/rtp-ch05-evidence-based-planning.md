# Chapter 5: Planning for Third Places Through Evidence-Based Urban Development

## Core Idea

Urban planning must reclaim public space for public use by treating third places as deliberate design outcomes, not accidental by-products of zoning. Cilliers argues that place-making, green planning, and lively planning already contain the ingredients planners need—but practitioners lack an integrative model that turns theory into evidence-based practice. The chapter synthesizes three planning traditions into four re-coded space types (social, economic, environmental, physical) and tests them against five international case studies to derive repeatable design priorities for third places.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Oldenburg criteria** — third places as regular, informal, voluntary gatherings outside home and work that frame public life and advance social justice.
- **Place-making (PPS)** — access, comfort and image, uses and activities, sociability as design and evaluation dimensions.
- **Green planning (Ahern et al.)** — multi-functionality, redundancy, diversity, multi-scale networks, adaptive planning for urban resilience.
- **Lively planning (Cilliers et al.)** — virtual, marketing, functional, environmental, social, psychological, sound, visual, and movement dimensions.
- **Integrative re-coding (Table 5.4)** — maps overlapping elements from all three approaches into four tiers: (1) social spaces, (2) economic spaces, (3) environmental spaces, (4) physical spaces.
- **Evidence-based practice (Krizek et al., Healey)** — case studies as bridges between formal research and professional decision-making.
- **Theory-based sampling (Glaser & Strauss)** — purposeful selection of planning approaches to generate actionable design guidance.

## Key Concepts

- **Public space for public use** — a normative stance against city-centre land-use competition that erodes qualitative gathering space.
- **Social friction** — beneficial interaction between groups who would otherwise not meet; place-making's social purpose.
- **Sense of place** — emotional belonging tied to appearance and function connected to space; appears in four of five cases.
- **Context-driven (cultural) design** — function must align with local user needs, not generic templates.
- **Nature-based solutions** — green infrastructure, colour, furniture, and enclosure used to enhance safety perceptions and aesthetic values.
- **Human-scale planning** — diversity of scale that respects pedestrian-level experience.
- **Quality social activities over quantity of facilities** — opportunities for social activity rank above physical equipment.

## Mental Models

- **Slow city / fast society paradox** — urban fabric changes slowly while community needs accelerate; planning mediates the mismatch.
- **Four-space stack** — every third-place intervention should score across social, economic, environmental, and physical layers, not optimize one dimension.
- **Frequency-weighted best practices** — design elements repeated across case studies (aesthetic values ×5, green initiatives ×4, sense of place ×4) signal non-negotiables.
- **Transform mono-functional to multi-functional** — parking lots, walk-through corridors, and neglected green edges become third places through temporary or permanent reprogramming.
- **Change spaces to change life** — third places are cross-cutting at every planning scale, not a specialty overlay.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating third places as policy-enforced or zoning-guaranteed—they often succeed *despite* weak legislative support.
- Over-indexing on **visibility** as a design driver; this research found it insignificant compared to aesthetics, sense of place, and green initiatives.
- Prioritizing **diverse uses** over **diverse users**—quality of who gathers matters more than programmatic variety.
- Equating **quality public facilities** with success when social activity opportunities matter far more.
- Applying place-making, green, or lively planning in isolation without mapping their interface.
- One-size-fits-all redesign that ignores context-driven cultural design and stakeholder-specific needs (children, university students, rural communities).

## Worked Example

Five LICI-linked transformations illustrate the integrative approach:

1. **Jardin de la Maison (Namur)** — neglected riverside green space redesigned with urban garden, colour, furniture, and river views; nature-based safety and enclosed soundscape.
2. **Louvain-La-Neuve** — university walk-through converted via snake-shaped seating (interrupting movement), CityTree initiative, green graffiti/roofs, mixed uses.
3. **Place du Cardinal Mercier (Wavre)** — central parking lot temporarily grassed as *Jardin Urbain* with play area, Wi-Fi, and community-wide activation.
4. **Doornakkers (Eindhoven)** — neighbourhood "new heart" linked by green corridors, walking routes, and diverse social/recreational functions.
5. **Ikageng (South Africa)** — functionless nursery yard become child-scale safe play space with colour and natural elements.

Synthesis (Table 5.10) ranks **aesthetic values**, **sense of place**, **green initiatives**, **quality social activities**, and **diverse users** as the top five design elements for planning third places.

## Key Takeaways

- Integrate place-making, green planning, and lively planning through the four-space re-coding before commissioning design.
- Lead with social analysis in design, not only in needs assessment—community is enforced through sense of place.
- Invest in nature-based aesthetics and green initiatives; they simultaneously deliver environmental, safety, and image benefits.
- Design for diverse users and quality social activities rather than equipment catalogs or use-mix checklists.
- Use evidence-based case evaluation (movement data, stakeholder analysis, ecological data) matched to each space tier.
- Accept that third places require holistic, interdisciplinary planning at city, neighbourhood, site, and regional scales.
