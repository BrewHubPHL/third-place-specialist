# Chapter 10: Third Places in Transit — Public Transport as a Third Place of Mobility

## Core Idea

O'Hare asks whether the public transport commute—historically a "home-to-work-and-back-again shuttle"—can function as a **third place of mobility** rather than dead time bracketed with work. Oldenburg set a high bar, but polycentric cities, portable work, and digital connectivity have blurred first/second/third distinctions. Commuters spend long minutes in shared vehicles and at stops; that time can be a **gift** rather than a cost if design, staffing, and adjacent urbanism support civility, choice, and serendipity.

Public transport nodes are **public places** (Nordahl, Walker): stations, platforms, and vehicles are civic space where third-place activity can flourish—or be engineered away by automation, advertising wraps, and cost-driven seat fixes.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Third place / "the great good place"** (Oldenburg) — Benchmark for transit as community, not just conveyance.
- **Travel time as gift vs. burden** (Jain and Lyons, 2008) — Reconceptualizing commute duration as usable social or restorative time.
- **Dwell within mobilities** (Lyons and Urry, 2005) — Blurring of travel time and activity time; passengers "at home" on the move via ICT.
- **Multiple Mobile Places** (Jensen, 2015) — Mobile others → mobile withs → mobile friends; routines and rhythms of the train produce third-place sociality.
- **Mehta and Bosson (2010) street third-place qualities** — Personalisation, permeability, seating, shelter applied to station precincts.
- **In-between mile approach / habitability paradigm** (Lavadhino, 2017) — Pedestrian-driven multimodal city; economy of place over economy of speed.
- **Parochialisation of public space** (Lofland, via Humphreys, 2010) — Location-aware social media makes transit space feel intimate.
- **Hybrid spaces** (Frith, 2013) — Merge of social connection, digital information, and physical place on the move.

## Key Concepts

- **Third place of mobility** — Commute as voluntary-in-practice social arena, not soulless interval.
- **Mobile second place for work** — Laptops, tray tables, Wi-Fi turn intercity rail into co-working; overlaps third-place relaxation and dining cars.
- **Civility** (Walker, 2012) — Rider respected as customer, citizen, and human; foundation for transit third places.
- **Custodians and curators** — Staff (e.g., Melbourne tram "connies") who host atmosphere; loss through automation.
- **Quiet carriages** — Passenger-enforced social contracts; culture-dependent compliance.
- **Flip seats / reversible seating** — User control over sociability and view; Sydney intercity controversy as third-place vs. cost tradeoff.
- **Eyes on the street** (Jacobs) — Passengers as passive surveillance; compromised by opaque advertising wrap on windows.
- **Serendipity** (Merton and Barber, 2003) — Chance encounters between diverse people at walkable nodes.

## Mental Models

1. **Trip = walk + wait + ride** — Third-place value lives in all segments, not only onboard.
2. **Gift framing** — Real-time info liberates street life en route to the stop (time for a chat, a beer, a detour).
3. **Transit as civic space** — Vehicles and stations deserve the same design care as streets and squares.
4. **Things to do, people to meet, places to dwell** (Lavadhino) — Grenoble parklet above the station as rendezvous for commuters, workers, children, isolated residents.
5. **Digital layer atop mobile layer** — Wi-Fi and location apps extend third place beyond the carriage into placeless networks.
6. **Planting social capital** — Station gardens and custodian hosts build ownership, reduce vandalism, create confidants.

## Anti-patterns

- **Office park + residential desert commute** — Edge-city monoculture with no lunchbreak third places within walking distance.
- **Staff stripping for efficiency** — Ticket machines and absent guards remove hospitality and security.
- **Economy of speed only** — Optimizing frequency and headways without habitable waiting environments.
- **Fixed seating against user preference** — Ignoring strong customer appeal of flip seats for maintenance and capacity.
- **Advertising wrap on windows** — Trading outward view, safety, and journey pleasure for marginal revenue.
- **Corporate station sameness** — System-wide identity at the expense of local personalisation and place attachment.
- **Officialdom vs. community gardening** — High-vis vests, chainsaws, and maintenance contracts destroying volunteer custodianship.
- **Disneyland-only transit** — Charm without reliability; "romantic slow transit" fails daily riders (Walker/Szarkowski critique of Nordahl).

## Worked Example

**Plamondon's Amtrak Cascades commute** (200 km, twice weekly): comfortable seats, tray tables, AC power, dining car for socializing or alternate workspace, friendly crew who knew him, station staff watching his parked car—rich third-place **and** work options. Contrast with typical 61-minute Sydney public transport commute vs. 26-minute car trip: mode choice often reflects privacy and status intolerance, not inevitability.

**Brisbane stationmaster's garden** (Begley, 2017): 30–45 minutes daily gardening; passengers contribute cuttings; reduced vandalism; social support in hard times—Oldenburg's **host** role in a perennial third place. System-wide replication never followed despite awards.

**Grenoble in-between mile** (Lavadhino): temporary interventions + apps; parklet above railway became picnic spot, play space, social hub for formerly isolated public-housing residents—third-place activities at the node, not only on the train.

## Key Takeaways

- Public transport **can** be a great good place when civility, comfort, and user control are designed in.
- **Blurred work/home/transit** means third-place criteria apply to mobile work and relaxation alike.
- **Station precincts** need Mehta-Bosson qualities plus walkable catchments; public transport is sparked by walking.
- **Romance and efficiency are not mutually exclusive** — frequency matters and experience matters.
- **Digital third places** (Facebook, Dodgeball/Snap Maps, gamification) parochialise transit space; Wi-Fi quality shapes what's possible onboard.
- **Distinctive system identity** (London Underground cultural landscape) builds public-transport community belonging.
- Research and implementation lag behind ICT potential—high-leverage area for practitioners.
