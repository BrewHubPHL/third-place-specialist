# Third-Place Thinking Principles

Five principles distilled from *Rethinking Third Places* (Dolley & Bosman, eds.) and Ray Oldenburg's foundational work. Apply these before endorsing any plan, brief, or development claim.

## 1. Conversation is the test, not the label

A space is not a third place because it is named "community hub," "town square," or "mixed-use centre." **Conversation must be the primary activity**, alongside neutral ground and genuine access. When in doubt, observe who talks to whom, for how long, and on what terms—not what the architect drew.

## 2. Planning enables or destroys; it rarely installs community

Master plans can supply walkable form, public furniture, and programming budgets. They cannot manufacture gemeinschaft. Prefer designs that allow **user modification** and weak-tie formation without scheduled events. Treat "community" marketing as a signal to map **exclusion** (price, gates, car need, participation rules).

## 3. Weak ties are infrastructure

Bridging social capital—acquaintances, nods, over-the-fence chats—supports ageing in place, newcomer integration, neighbourhood safety perception, and information flow. Bonding-only spaces (locked clubs, homogeneous gated commons) have value but **do not substitute** for porous third places when integration is the goal.

## 4. No place is neutral

Third places are shaped by gender, race, class, ability, austerity politics, and platform governance. Audit **emplacement**: who feels entitled to linger, who performs unpaid labour, who is surveilled or excluded. "Inclusive" rhetoric without intersectional audit is an anti-pattern.

## 5. Layer, don't replace

Physical third places remain the anchor for face-to-face public life. Digital layers, AR, heritage archives, and transit-time sociality can extend reach—but when mediated interaction becomes primary, or platform code excludes more easily than physical design, the third-place function erodes. Evaluate every layer against Oldenburg's eight characteristics.
